Basement Water Damage Cleanup in Tarzana, CA

Lower levels collect water first and dry last, so the plan is built around pumping, concrete and the drainage behind it.

Call (818) 337-2642

An honest note first. True basements are less common in this part of Los Angeles than in colder parts of the country, and we cannot give you a count. What Tarzana homes do have are lower levels, such as below-grade garages, a lower floor cut into a hillside slope, rooms behind a retaining wall and the occasional older house with a cellar. This page covers water in all of those spaces.

The work follows the same logic as any water loss. Stop the source, pump out the water, remove what cannot be saved and dry the rest. Lower levels add concrete, drainage and poor air flow, which slow every step.

Where the water in a lower level comes from

Most lower-level water comes from one of three places. Plumbing is the first, and it includes a failed water heater, a supply line or a washer hose. Surface water is the second, and it arrives from a slope, a patio or a downspout that dumps beside the wall. Groundwater seepage through a slab or wall joint is the third.

The source decides who fixes it and how insurance may see it. A plumbing failure is a repair job. Runoff and seepage are drainage jobs that restoration does not fix.

What the city treats as a basement

The Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety general notes for single-family homes require smoke alarms on each story and basement of multi-story dwellings and carbon monoxide alarms on every level, including basements. That tells you the city treats a basement as a living level and not only storage.

For you, a wet lower level is a wet living space. Ask LADBS before you alter drainage, walls or a retaining wall, because permit rules depend on the address.

Emergency or can it wait? Reading the water

Standing water that is rising, touching electrical equipment or spreading through stored belongings should not wait. Damp concrete with no pooled water is slower, but it still needs a plan, because moisture trapped in a closed lower room can start mold growth.

First-hour safety steps for a wet lower level

Do not walk into standing water if the lower level has outlets, a panel, a freezer or a sump pump running. Shut off the main water valve if a pipe is the source. If the water is above the outlets, leave it and call 911 and the utility. Watch the stairs, because wet concrete and treads are slick and the water can hide steps.

How we clean up a flooded lower level

Water gets pumped out first, usually with a submersible pump and hoses that carry it away from the house. Pumping too fast against wet soil outside can stress a wall, so crews work in stages. After the standing water is gone, the structure is the focus.

  1. Remove wet carpet, pad and stored items that cannot be dried.
  2. Make a flood cut, which is a straight cut through the drywall a few inches above the visible water line, so wet drywall and insulation below it can come out.
  3. Open the bottom of the wall cavity and check the framing with a moisture meter.
  4. Clean and sanitize hard surfaces, especially if the water touched a drain or the ground.
  5. Set air movers and dehumidifiers and run them until concrete, framing and remaining drywall read dry.

Concrete is the slow part. It holds moisture and releases it gradually, so a floor that looks dry can still be giving off vapor. The drying equipment is described in more detail on the structural drying and dehumidification page.

Odor and what to keep

Lower rooms hold smell. Once wet materials are out and surfaces are clean, air scrubbers with HEPA filtration and steady ventilation help clear stale odor. Cardboard, soft furniture and anything porous that sat in water for a day or more is often better discarded than treated.

Photograph and list what you remove before it goes out the door. Your adjuster may ask for it.

What affects the price of basement water cleanup

Lower-level jobs are priced from the facts of the space. A crew assesses it before giving a figure.

  • Water depth and volumeMore water means more pumping time and more equipment.
  • Water categoryRain runoff or sewage adds removal and sanitizing steps that clean pipe water does not.
  • Finished versus unfinishedA finished room has drywall, flooring and trim to remove and later replace.
  • Concrete and drying timeSlabs and block walls dry slowly and extend equipment days.
  • AccessNarrow stairs and long hose runs add labor.

Scope and price are explained before work starts. Describe the space and the water, and ask what the crew would plan to remove.

Insurance and lower-level water

Insurance guidance from the state separates flood from other water losses, and flood is written on a different policy. A burst pipe in a lower level is a plumbing question, while rising surface water may fall on the flood side.

Check your policy and tell your insurer early. The Sepulveda Dam in Encino is a flood control structure, and the Army Corps of Engineers describes it as vital for flood risk management of portions of the San Fernando Valley, with the Los Angeles River flowing into it. It is a regional control, not a promise that any single lot stays dry, so look up your own address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center.

Prevention and mistakes to avoid

  • Keep downspouts discharging away from the walls of a lower level.
  • Store valuables on shelves and not on the floor.
  • Do not run a heater near a wet lower room to dry it, because heat without dehumidification only moves moisture.
  • Do not wait on musty smells, which signal trapped moisture.
  • Do not assume a dry-looking slab is dry.
