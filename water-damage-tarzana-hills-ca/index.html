The Tarzana Hills is a local way of describing the hillside homes south of Ventura Boulevard, in the foothills of the Santa Monica Mountains. Water behaves differently there than on the valley floor, because a slope sends runoff toward and around houses and because homes are often built on several levels.

This page explains what the hillside setting changes about a water loss, what official sources say about the terrain, and what to do first. It covers water damage inside the home and does not offer slope repair, grading or waterproofing.

What we mean by the Tarzana Hills

Tarzana Hills is an informal name and not an officially bounded district. Local real estate guides use Ventura Boulevard as the practical dividing line between the flat streets to the north and the hillside streets to the south, and we use the same line. Exact edges are approximate.

City Planning figures give a sense of the terrain. In a City of Los Angeles hillside planning document, more than half of the Encino-Tarzana Community Plan Area is described as hillside, and most of that hillside lies south of Ventura Boulevard.

How a slope changes water damage

On a hillside lot, water can enter from uphill, from a roof valley, from a failed drain or from a plumbing line. Because the ground slopes, water that reaches a lower level can collect against walls and flow along floor edges.

Split-level and multi-level homes add another pattern. Water that starts on an upper floor can travel down through ceilings, wall cavities and stair openings, so a stain in a lower room may come from a leak two floors above. A crew should trace the path before drying one room only.

Access also matters. Narrow hillside streets, gated driveways and limited guest parking can slow a crew carrying equipment, so describe the approach when you call.

What official sources say about hillside storms

Federal geologists studied the storms of January 1969 in the Santa Monica Mountains. Their report tied debris flows to rain falling faster than about a fifth to a quarter of an inch per hour, and to slopes already soaked by roughly 10 inches earlier in the season.

That is a historical study of the mountains and not a prediction for any one street. It does show why heavy rain on saturated slopes is treated differently from a light shower. We do not claim any Tarzana Hills street has flooded or slid, because we found no record to support it.

The Encino-Tarzana Community Plan also directs that hillside development have adequate sewers and drainage facilities, which shows drainage is a planning concern in these areas.

Safety first: the first hour on a hillside lot

Close the main water valve only if you can reach it without wading. Stay out of standing water near outlets or cords, and treat runoff from a slope or a drain as dirty.

Do not walk on a saturated slope, a retaining wall or an unstable patch of ground. If you see mud moving, hear a wall or hillside shifting, or smell gas, leave and call 911. Photograph the damage from a safe spot, then lift valuables off wet floors.

Extraction and drying on multi-level homes

A crew first removes standing water, then checks moisture in floors, walls and ceilings with meters. Air movers and dehumidifiers are set so air circulates through wet materials, and readings are repeated until the structure is back to normal levels. The written reference for this process is ANSI/IICRC S500, named here as a reference and not as a certification.

Because water can sit in lower walls and under flooring, drying often takes more than one visit. A written scope that lists rooms, materials and equipment helps you see what was done and why.

Insurance documentation and flood coverage

Coverage depends on the cause of the damage and your policy. State insurance guidance separates flood, which needs its own flood policy, from other water damage that a homeowners policy can include.

That distinction matters on a hillside, where runoff and a plumbing failure can look alike. Photos, moisture readings and an itemized scope can help describe the loss, but they do not guarantee reimbursement. Ask your insurer before work begins.
